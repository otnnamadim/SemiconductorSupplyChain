The AI Semiconductor Taxonomy: Overview

Aperture Insights · Published on August 14, 2026

An AI server rack is not one chip but a stack of specialized ones, each drawn from a different branch of the semiconductor family tree. This page takes the taxonomy in Figure 3 — the Digital / Analog / Discrete structure of AI silicon — and, for each node, names the 2–5 public companies that lead it, the market driver that sets demand for that block, and how it fits the ecosystem around it. The organizing spine is the diagram's own, not a flat four-bucket list: logic and memory sit under Digital; signal and power under Analog; optoelectronics, power transistors, and sensors under Discrete.

Read it as a map of where the AI-infrastructure dollar lands as it moves from the grid, through power conversion, into compute and memory, and back out across the network. A company can occupy more than one node — Nvidia is compute and networking; Broadcom is custom compute and networking and optics; Infineon spans analog power and discrete transistors — so these are functional positions, not clean corporate boxes.

This is a summarized educational map of market structure, not investment advice and not a recommendation of any security. Company placements are indicative of business focus and role within the ecosystem and not an exhaustive list. Jump to Digital Memory / Storage Analog Discrete

Digital · Logic

Chips that compute, control, and move binary data. In this branch the AI dollar is largest and growth is steepest — it contains the accelerators that do the training and inference, the host processors that orchestrate them, and (per the report's Note 3) the networking silicon that ties racks together.

Parallel accelerators GPUs · AI accelerators · ASICs · FPGAs

The engine room of the buildout: chips that run thousands of operations at once for training and inference. The defining contest is merchant GPU vs. custom ASIC — Nvidia's general-purpose platform against hyperscaler-specific silicon designed by Broadcom and Marvell.

Serial processors CPUs · host / orchestration

General-purpose chips that execute sequentially and orchestrate the accelerators — every GPU tray still needs a host CPU to schedule work and manage I/O. Smaller and slower-growing than the accelerator node, but structurally attached to it: accelerator units sold pull CPU attach with them.

Digital · Memory / Storage

Stores, retrieves, and feeds data to compute. The AI-critical distinction sits inside this branch: HBM (fast memory packaged beside the GPU) is the true bottleneck and the pricing power; NAND is bulk storage for the datasets — related, but a different rung with different economics.

Memory makers HBM · DRAM · NAND · SRAM

A near-consolidated field. The scarce, margin-rich product is HBM — capacity has been effectively sold out, and each new GPU generation stacks more of it. That scarcity is why memory has swung from commodity to kingmaker in the AI cycle.

Demand driver  HBM: bytes and bandwidth per accelerator — set directly by GPU roadmaps (more stacks, taller stacks each generation). NAND: the size of the datasets being retained and served. HBM is capacity-gated, so it behaves less like a commodity and more like a co-designed component of the GPU itself.

Analog

Chips that regulate power, manage heat, and convert real-world signals for the digital infrastructure. Lower unit prices than accelerators, but high attach counts — a rack needs many of these, and as power density climbs, the power sub-node has become a genuine AI story rather than a sleepy analog line.

Power management PMICs · voltage regulators

Converts and delivers clean, high-current power to the GPU at the point of load. As accelerators draw more current at lower voltages, the precision and density of this delivery becomes a gating constraint — and the content per GPU rises with it.

Signal & interface data converters · clocks · timing

Manages the boundary between analog reality and digital logic — converting signals and keeping chips synchronized. As data rates climb, precise timing and signal integrity move from afterthought to enabler; this is the node the flat "four-bucket" view of the market usually omits.

Demand driver  Link speeds and I/O density — every jump in data rate raises the bar for conversion, clocking, and signal integrity. Demand tracks the number and speed of high-speed interfaces in the rack, not just the number of GPUs.

Discrete Devices

Standalone components that each perform one function. Two sub-nodes carry real AI weight: optoelectronics, where the photonics interconnect story lives, and power transistors, where the shift to high-voltage rack power is playing out. Sensors round out the branch as the infrastructure's monitoring layer.

Optoelectronics photonics · optical interconnect

Devices that convert electrical signals to light and back — the substrate of optical interconnect. As copper hits its reach-and-power limits between racks, optics (and co-packaged optics) become structural, not optional. Nvidia has committed on the order of $4B to secure this supply, a signal of how load-bearing the node has become.

Power transistors GaN · SiC · discrete power

Single-function devices that switch and convert high power efficiently. The catalyst here is the move to 800V HVDC rack architectures — wide-bandgap materials (GaN, SiC) that carry AI-scale power with far less copper and loss than legacy silicon.

Demand driver  The 800V power transition and total rack wattage — higher voltage cuts copper and loss, and wide-bandgap devices are what make it work. Note the cross-current: as an emerging theme, the near-term 800V TAM is debated even as the long-run direction is clear.

Sensors & transducers monitoring · current sensing · thermal

Devices that detect and convert physical signals into data — the monitoring layer of the data center. Less directly tied to AI compute than the other discrete nodes, but rising with the buildout as denser, hotter, higher-power racks need more telemetry to run safely.

How to read the map

Follow one dollar of AI-infrastructure spend and it traces the taxonomy in order: it enters through discrete power transistors converting grid power, is refined by analog power management at the board, spent mostly on digital accelerators and HBM memory doing the work, and routed back out through networking and optics to the next rack — with signal, timing, and sensors keeping the whole thing coherent. The value concentrates heavily at the compute and memory nodes; the surrounding nodes are smaller per unit but attach in high multiples and, for networking and optics, grow faster than GPU count as clusters get larger.

Two structural cautions. First, leadership here is a matter of degree and moves — HBM qualification, ASIC design wins, and the 800V transition are all live contests, not settled ones. Second, several names span nodes, so a single company's exposure to "AI" is a blend across this map rather than a single line. Upstream of everything shown sits the equipment and foundry layer (ASML's lithography, TSMC's fabrication) that makes every one of these chips physically possible.
